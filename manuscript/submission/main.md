# A co-essentiality score selected on training complexes wins a single held-out test but yields neither better-supported complexes nor more replicable complex-level dependencies in DepMap

**Authors:** [AUTHORS]

---

## Abstract

Co-essentiality methods for inferring protein complexes are often tuned and judged on overlapping curated labels. In a pre-registered study, an unsupervised co-complex score, configured on training complexes and locked before the hu.MAP 3.0 test files arrived, was evaluated once. Average precision was 0.333, against 0.212 for generalised least squares (GLS); the gain was concentrated in pair-rich clusters, and macro per-cluster AP did not differ. A map built from the score on 1,007 DepMap lines agreed with BioPlex no better than a GLS map (primary bridge), and its context associations confirmed less often in 338 held-out lines (secondary bridge; −0.065, 95% CI −0.107 to −0.025). Gene sets from other co-dependency modules, matched for size, essentiality and coherence, associated with lineages as often as complexes (28.1% of tests each); essentiality-only sets did so less (16.6%, post hoc). About half of 1,145 lineage associations (selective FDR) confirmed, as post hoc power predicts.

---

## Introduction

Genome-scale CRISPR loss-of-function screens across cancer cell lines, such as the Cancer Dependency Map (DepMap), measure the fitness effect of knocking out each gene in each line [Tsherniak 2017; Meyers 2017; Dempster 2021 (Chronos); Arafeh 2025]. Subunits of a protein complex tend to have correlated fitness profiles, so co-essentiality is used to recover complexes, pathways and the functions of uncharacterised genes [Pan 2018; Boyle 2018; Kim 2019; Wainberg 2021]. Generalised least squares (GLS) on whitened profiles is a widely used standard for building co-essential modules [Wainberg 2021].

Two problems limit how far such results can be trusted. Method development and evaluation often share the curated labels used to judge success, and pair-input predictions are optimistic when test pairs share proteins with training pairs [Park & Marcotte 2012]. Downstream complex-level analyses of cancer dependencies often start from curated resources (CORUM [Tsitsiridis 2023], Complex Portal [Meldal 2022]) or functional networks (STRING [Szklarczyk 2023]), which makes it hard to separate what the dependency data show from what curation encoded.

We address both with a pre-registered, two-part design. In Part 1, a co-complex score developed and locked in earlier work (zavg_BEF) was evaluated once on withheld hu.MAP 3.0 test pairs [Fischer 2025] against Pearson, Spearman and GLS. The score has no parameters fitted to labels, but its configuration was selected on hu.MAP training complexes. In Part 2, the locked score was recomputed on a construction subset of DepMap lines and clustered into a complex map without reading any curated complex resource, functional network or hu.MAP prediction. We call this map curation-free in that narrow sense only: curated information enters indirectly through the score's configuration, and the positive controls encode literature knowledge. BioPlex 3.0 [Huttlin 2021] and CCLE proteomics [Nusinow 2020], read only after the map was frozen, tiered it, and two registered "bridges" compared it with a GLS map. Context-specific dependency analyses on the map were read against registered calibrations and confirmed in a single read of held-out cell lines.

The pair-level advantage is real but did not translate into better-supported complexes or more replicable complex-level dependencies. Context associations of complexes are matched by those of other coherent co-dependency modules, and they confirm at about the rate power predicts. We also report a defect in the confirmation code found after the read, and the registered strongest findings with the revision's post hoc checks.

## Results

### The locked score wins a single pre-registered test

The Part 1 method was locked 59.6 min before the hu.MAP 3.0 test files reached the project (the test split itself was published earlier [Fischer 2025]); an independent audit found no evidence that test pairs, labels or test-derived information entered Part 1. All contact with the test files before the protocol freeze is disclosed (Supplementary Note 1). One incident is material: an audit agent counted test pairs in Part 1's table of pairs with zavg_BEF ≥ 3, so that operating point is non-blind and enters no verdict. Because genome-wide scores already covered the test pairs, the single-look guarantee is procedural. The test set is pair-disjoint from training, and 2,309 of its 3,312 positives have neither protein among training-positive proteins (stratum C3).

The protocol was frozen with a marker file hashing the protocol, fitted rules, calibrators, evaluation code and test files, and the look ran once. On all 600,738 canonical test pairs (3,312 positives; intention-to-score, ITT), zavg_BEF reached average precision (AP) 0.333 (95% cluster-bootstrap CI 0.256–0.410), against 0.212 (0.156–0.273) for GLS, 0.204 (0.106–0.352) for Spearman and 0.197 (0.103–0.338) for Pearson (Fig. 1b; Table 1). The paired differences were +0.121 (+0.079 to +0.159), +0.129 (+0.023 to +0.216) and +0.136 (+0.039 to +0.220). CIs come from the protocol's paired cluster bootstrap over 238 test clusters (B = 2,000), whose Kish effective number is 21.3 [Kish 1965, M12]. The locked "clearly beats" rule, computed on pairs mapped under every method (S1, 3,159 positives), was met against all three baselines. An agent that had not written the evaluation code re-implemented the protocol and reproduced every verdict-relevant number to ≤ 2.6 × 10⁻¹⁵. The gain held on protein-new pairs (C3: AP 0.420 against 0.272 for GLS; +0.148, +0.093 to +0.199). Operating points and calibration are in Supplementary Note 10.

The gain is not uniform. Macro per-cluster AP, which weights each test cluster equally, was 0.297 for zavg_BEF and 0.292 for GLS (+0.005, −0.014 to +0.047) (Fig. 1d). In a descriptive breakdown made after the look, macro AP differed from GLS's by −0.033 (−0.090 to +0.023) for the 91 clusters with one positive pair and by +0.142 (+0.078 to +0.198) for the 6 clusters with more than 100 (Fig. 1f). The locked score ranks pairs in large complexes better and shows no advantage on the smallest ones. Two analyses the referee requested, a preprocessing-matched GLS baseline and leave-one-large-cluster-out ΔAP, would require scoring the test set again and were not run (Supplementary Note 17).

### A complex map, and a null primary bridge

Before any dependency value was read, the 1,345 non-engineered DepMap lines were split into a lockbox L of 338 lines and a construction set A of 1,007, PatientID-blocked and stratified by lineage and genotype. The score was recomputed on A over the 6,022 genes with a dependency in at least 1% of A lines (S). Mutual top-k edges above a score threshold z0 were clustered by consensus Leiden clustering over PatientID-blocked subsamples of A (Methods). A registered split-half statistic chose z0 = 6 and k = 10 (0.343), but it was flat across z0 (0.341 at z0 = 4), so z0 is weakly determined (Fig. 2c).

The map has 367 level-1 complexes covering 3,642 genes (median size 9); a GLS map at matched edge count has 379 complexes covering 2,862 genes (median 5) (Fig. 2a; Table 2). Clusters with at least half their members within 10 Mb or on one chromosome arm, the signature of copy-number and CRISPR-cutting artefacts [Aguirre 2016; Munoz 2016; Lazar 2024], are flagged as locus clusters and excluded from biological claims: 28.3% of zavg and 22.2% of GLS complexes. Of the 367 zavg complexes, 159 are T1 (BioPlex edges above a degree- and essentiality-preserving null), 74 T2 (protein co-abundance above a size-matched null) and 134 neither (Fig. 2d). "Complex" below means a physically or proteomically supported co-dependency module; some merge two complexes or are pathways. Rebuilt on each subsample, the map recovered the typical complex with median best-match Jaccard 0.69, against 0.06 for random size-matched sets; the GLS map was about as stable (0.67) (Fig. 2e).

**The primary bridge is null.** The registered primary endpoint compared size-standardised within-cluster BioPlex precision at matched gene coverage. Precision was 0.157 for zavg and 0.182 for GLS, a difference of −0.025 (−0.075 to +0.031) (Fig. 2f). The hypothesis that the zavg map agrees better with physical evidence is not supported. The interval is also not narrow: it is compatible with an advantage of up to 0.031 for zavg or 0.075 for GLS, so the result is absence of evidence of a difference, not evidence of equivalence. With locus clusters excluded (a sensitivity fixed before validation) the difference was +0.003 (−0.060 to +0.074). Protein co-abundance, a secondary endpoint, favoured GLS (−0.061, −0.101 to −0.018), while at the pair level the locked score still ranked BioPlex pairs better (AP 0.0345 against 0.0215; no CI).

### Positive controls pass the kill rule

Twenty-three positive controls were registered before the map existed (Supplementary Table S1; the design numbers them PC1–PC22 and registers PC19 as two controls), each mapped to a direction and run once on A. A direction is dropped if fewer than half of its evaluable complex-level controls are recovered. All 15 evaluable complex-level controls were recovered: H3a 2 of 2, H3b 10 of 10 and H1 3 of 3 (for example WRN in MSI-H lines, PRMT5 with 9p21 loss, PELO–HBS1L with FOCAD loss [Chan 2019; Kryukov 2016; Prindle 2025]). The rule is lenient and the controls are strong, well-known dependencies, so passing it shows that the machinery recovers known biology, not that it has power for new effects. H2 has no evaluable control and is exploratory.

### Context associations are matched by other coherent co-dependency modules

Each direction was run once on A after a committed intent. In the lineage atlas (H3a; one-versus-rest lineage contexts × 367 complexes, m = 8,441), 1,145 tests were discoveries under Benjamini–Bogomolov selective FDR at q = 0.05, in 21 of 23 families; 942 lie outside locus clusters. With 21 of 23 families selected, this is close to within-family BH at q ≈ 0.046. In the driver and genome-state family (H3b; 20 contexts, m = 7,340), 161 tests were discoveries. The controlled quantity is the within-family false discovery proportion averaged over selected families; the pooled proportion was 0.068 in a simulation that planted lineage effects only, and no simulation covered the H3b dependence structure. Discoveries concentrate in large lineages (Lymphoid 197) and in TP53 alteration (92). Effects are mostly small: 325 of the 1,145 H3a discoveries have |β| < 0.05 Chronos units.

**Matched gene sets associate as often as complexes.** The registered calibration scores and tests ten gene sets per complex, matched for size, essentiality and coherence, exactly like the complex in every context. The sets are drawn from the members of other complexes, so they are fragments of other co-dependency modules. They reached p < 0.05 as often as the complexes: 28.1% of tests against 28.1% in H3a and 11.6% against 12.0% in H3b (Fig. 3a). Applied to the matched-set tests, the same selective FDR procedure made 11,861 rejections from 80,500 tests (14.7%), against 1,145 from 8,441 (13.6%) for the complexes. Seventeen complexes had no matched sets and 12 needed doubled tolerances. These calibrations were registered but implemented only after the discovery runs, before any lockbox read (Table 3).

Two post hoc reference analyses, run on A only for this revision, show what the parity does and does not mean (Fig. 6a; Supplementary Note 17). First, coherent co-dependency neighbourhoods of genes that belong to no complex could be matched for only 90 of the 367 complexes, the less coherent ones. On those 90, they reached p < 0.05 in 25.1% of H3a tests, against 23.0% for the complexes; sets matched on size and essentiality but not coherence reached 16.4%. The H3b values were 10.8%, 11.3% and 8.4%. Over all 355 complexes with such sets, essentiality-only sets reached 16.6% against 28.1%. **A lineage contrast therefore moves coherent, essentiality-matched co-dependency modules about as much as complexes, and moves essentiality-matched sets without coherence less.** Sets matched for coherence behave like complexes, and sets matched for essentiality alone associate less often. We do not attribute the excess to coherence: coherence is itself measured on A as co-variation across lines, so matching on it partly conditions on the outcome. The comparison also rests on the less coherent quarter of the map.

Second, the flag that defined "complex-specific" in the first version, a discovery whose |t| exceeds all ten of its matched sets, compared selected discoveries with unselected sets. When ten random atlases, each replacing every complex by one of its matched sets, went through the full discovery procedure, 32.9% of their H3a discoveries beat all ten other members of their group (29.1–36.4% across the ten atlases), against 46.4% of real discoveries (H3b: 48.3%, range 44.8–51.7%, against 66.9%) (Fig. 6b). No atlas reached the real rate. Selection alone thus lifts the flag from the 9.1% expected without selection to about a third. Conditional on discovery, real complexes beat their own matched sets somewhat more often, but their |t| was not larger than that of random-atlas discoveries in the same context (mean probability 0.48; |β|, 0.49). The excess is relative to a complex's own matched sets, not an effect-size advantage, and we no longer call flagged discoveries complex-specific.

**Two lineage-level artefacts.** Dispersed sets of genes expressed in no RNA-profiled A line reached p < 0.05 in 13.5% of H3a tests, concentrated in melanoma, Skin and Lymphoid (Fig. 3e). A lineage contrast thus shifts even gene sets with no expression, which points to a lineage-level shift of the CRISPR effect scale. The registered response is tied to the pseudo-context rate (4.9%), so it did not fire; that literal reading was fixed after the discovery outcomes were known and before the calibrations ran. Second, in Lymphoid the effect on a complex correlates with its essentiality (Spearman ρ = −0.240 over 367 complexes), consistent with a compressed Chronos dynamic range [Dempster 2021] (Fig. 3b). Several lineages show trends of the opposite sign, for example Skin (+0.207) and Head and Neck (+0.202). Screen-quality covariates kept 843 of 1,145 H3a and 115 of 161 H3b discoveries (Supplementary Note 13).

### Within-complex genetic interactions: known biology and small changes

H1 asks whether genetic loss of a gene changes dependency on the rest of its complex: a paralogue in the complex may become more essential, the other members may become more or less essential, or nothing may change beyond ±0.1 Chronos units (neutral, by two one-sided tests [Schuirmann 1987]). Two amendments made from event counts and labels before any H1 run defined the panel (Methods). The panel has 24 events with 26 tests, and 5 were discoveries.

The three non-neutral calls recover registered or published biology (Fig. 4a–c; Supplementary Table S4). In 37 ARID1A-loss lines, ARID1B became more essential (β_P = −0.374, 95% CI −0.512 to −0.237), the registered control PC6 [Helming 2014]. In 12 EP300-loss lines, at the evaluability floor, CREBBP became more essential (β_P = −1.093, −1.462 to −0.725), the reciprocal of a published dependency [Ogiwara 2016]. In 30 FOCAD-loss lines, the other members became more essential (κ_N = +0.140, +0.085 to +0.194), carried by PELO and HBS1L, the dependency registered as control PC10 [Prindle 2025]. TP53 (κ_N = +0.056) and PTEN (−0.057) losses were detectable but within the margin and are not headlined.

For 11 of the 24 events, the mean complex-wide (non-paralog) dependency changed by less than ±0.1 Chronos units (90% CI inside the margin). One of them is ARID1A, whose paralogue ARID1B did change, so 10 events showed no change on any tested axis. The margin is registered but loose relative to the context effects: 325 H3a discoveries are smaller than half of it. "Neutral" therefore means no change larger than 0.1 Chronos units, not no change. At a ±0.05 margin none of the 11 would be neutral: no committed 90% CI lies inside it, and the smallest margin containing each CI ranges from 0.059 to 0.097 (post hoc, from the committed H1 table). The neutral call therefore depends entirely on the registered ±0.1 margin. The registered report of the margin in residual-SD units needs each fit's residual SD, which was not exported, and is not given. Sub-module divergence (H2, exploratory) was mostly reproduced by random member splits (Supplementary Note 12).

### The lockbox read, and a defect in the confirmation code

After a dry check, three pre-read amendments, a code review and a dress rehearsal on A, the 30 registered entries were read once each, in registry order, in 2.6 h. After the read we found that the driver built the L covariate design over all models before subsetting to L. As a result, 53 fits failed (50 because of the defect) and 510 successful fits carried one spurious column, with small shifts (median |Δβ| 2.4 × 10⁻⁴). The rehearsal had shown the failures, but nobody checked fit status. H3a is unaffected; of the 28 failed H3b fits, 26 are in WGD. Both rulings were made after the read: the read as executed stays the primary result, a corrected relook of all 560 affected fits is reported alongside as non-confirmatory, and **every call that differs between the two (53 calls) is labelled defect-sensitive and supports no claim** (Supplementary Note 3). An independent reviewer recomputed 54 calls with separate code under both designs, and all agreed.

### About half the associations confirm, as power predicts

Of 1,145 H3a discoveries, 601 were confirmed in L (52.5%). The H3b rate is 0.49 among evaluable fits (60 of 122), 0.40 counting all fits (60 of 150) and 0.46 in the corrected relook (69 of 150) (Fig. 5a; Supplementary Table S6); because the failed fits are concentrated in WGD, none of the three should be read alone. Discoveries are selected for large A estimates, and L has about a third of A's lines. A post hoc power aid, computed after the read and not a test, predicts 0.47–0.52 for H3a and 0.43–0.44 for H3b once A effects are shrunk by an empirical-Bayes posterior [Efron 2011] (Fig. 5a). Confirmation is close to this rate (H3a slightly above it). Discoveries that beat all their matched sets confirmed more often (0.572 against 0.489), but power predicts most of that gap (0.52–0.53 against 0.41–0.43). The aid treats the shrunken A effects as the true L effects. Gene-set-generic and scale effects would replicate in L too, so agreement with power cannot separate biology from reproducible artefacts. L lines were also part of the data on which zavg_BEF was developed and were processed jointly with A in one DepMap release, so confirmation establishes reproducibility within that release, not independence from shared artefacts.

### The secondary bridge: GLS-map discoveries confirm more often

BRIDGE-L, the registered secondary bridge, asks whether discoveries made on the zavg map are confirmed in L more often than those made with identical machinery on the GLS map, within matched size bins. Among evaluable hits (zavg 1,267, GLS 1,326), the size-standardised rate was 0.514 against 0.578, **a difference of −0.065 (95% CI −0.107 to −0.025)**, opposite to the hypothesis (Fig. 5d). It holds at matched gene coverage (−0.074, −0.122 to −0.026).

We applied the power aid to both maps, as for complex-specificity (post hoc; Fig. 6d). GLS-map discoveries have larger A effects (median |β| 0.081 against 0.066), so they are expected to confirm slightly more often: the expected difference is −0.023 (SD 0.019) under the headline model. Across three versions of the power model, power accounts for roughly a quarter to two fifths of the observed difference (expected −0.017 to −0.026 against −0.065). The remainder, −0.042, is about 2.2 SDs beyond expectation; this SD treats hits as independent, so the figure is approximate. The headline model also under-predicts both maps' confirmation levels (zavg by 0.055, GLS by 0.097). The negative result is therefore partly, not wholly, a power difference. The same caution applies to complex-specificity, where power accounted for most of the gap.

### Complex scores against their best gene: inconclusive

BENCH asks whether a complex score replicates in L better than the member that looked best in A. Δ is the difference between their L t-statistics over 1,295 discovery pairs. In the registered high-breadth tercile, Δ was +0.240 (60% of pairs favour the complex), with a bootstrap CI of −0.033 to +0.362. The bootstrap is compromised: 16.5% of pair × replicate fits failed silently, 1,032 of 2,000 replicates averaged over a reduced set of pairs, and with the covariate design assembled within each replicate the lower bound is −0.122. **We regard the registered interval as unreliable and the result as inconclusive, with a point estimate favouring the complex.** The persisted, hash-checked lockbox reads would allow a bootstrap with an explicit failure policy; we chose not to make this second, post hoc look at L, to keep the single-read design, so the registered result stays inconclusive. In the low tercile the best gene replicated better (Δ −0.338), which is largely expected, because breadth is defined by how many members are themselves associated.

### The registered strongest findings

The registry defines the strongest findings in advance: discovery q < 0.05; confirmed in the primary read or passed the rare-context route (a check, for contexts with too few L lines, that re-uses A lines and DEMETER2 and is not an independent replication); not defect-sensitive; not a locus cluster; and robust to leaving out each primary disease (H3a) or lineage (H3b, H1). The leave-one-out checks were registered but computed only after the lockbox read, on A, against a candidate list committed first. **254 findings meet the rule: 207 lineage associations, 45 driver or genome-state associations and 2 H1 calls** (Supplementary Table S9). The leave-one-out check could be applied fully to 199 of them and only partly to 49; 6 passed the rare-context route instead of a lockbox test (EP300 → CREBBP and five EWSR1-fusion findings). The check is strict where one disease dominates a lineage (Bowel kept none of 29 candidates) and vacuous where the remainder has fewer than two lines (Pancreas passes 17 of 17). After the run, the PI resolved two ambiguous registry readings (the H1 leave-one-out unit; ranking by tier, then |t|). Under the alternative readings (post hoc, from committed tables), only the two H1 calls could change, so the list would hold 252–254 findings; whether either call changes cannot be determined without a refit. Ranking selects nothing, so the alternative rankings change only the order (Supplementary Note 16).

To pick examples for the text we used post hoc labels, which are a presentation choice, not a test. The first version applied a lineage-scale label only in Lymphoid and Myeloid and used a linear trend. The revised rule fits a smooth (spline) trend of β on unit essentiality in every lineage. It labels 95 of the 207 H3a findings a possible scale artefact (sign as the trend predicts, within two residual SDs), against 56 before. It leaves 37 lead examples (T1, not a control readout, at least two residual SDs from the trend). **Of these 37, 26 lie beyond the trend in the trend's own direction, so the rule cannot exclude a scale artefact for them**; four lie within 0.05 of the threshold. Nine are in Lymphoid or melanoma, where never-expressed gene sets reach p < 0.05 in 27% and 33% of tests, so we treat those nine as less reliable. Leads in other lineages are not thereby reliable: there the rate ranges from 1% (Myeloid) to 22%.

As an orthogonal check that is not subject to Chronos's dynamic range, we refitted every H3a finding on DEMETER2 RNAi data for the 423 A lines that have it (post hoc; Fig. 6c). For 29 of 35 evaluable leads the sign agreed, 20 had one-sided p < 0.05, and the rank correlation of effects was 0.86. Six leads were discordant: Soft Tissue Z0080 and Z0336, Kidney Z0323, Pancreas Z0283, and the iron–sulfur unit Z0001 in Lung and Bone. Over all 207 H3a findings, 157 of 194 agreed in sign.

## Discussion

This study tested one co-essentiality score through two registered stages: a single look at withheld co-complex pairs, and a single read of held-out cell lines for every complex-level claim built on it.

**The method wins the test it was built for, within a defined scope.** On held-out hu.MAP 3.0 complexes, zavg_BEF beat GLS, Spearman and Pearson under a decision rule frozen before the look, also on protein-new pairs. The gain is carried by complexes with many positive pairs; per cluster, the score is not better. Its configuration was selected on training labels derived from Complex Portal [Fischer 2025], so agreement with curated complexes partly re-measures that selection. A post hoc PC-removed Pearson reference closes part of the gap to Pearson (Supplementary Note 10), so preprocessing probably contributes to the gain; we could not test a preprocessing-matched GLS without a second look at the test set.

**The advantage did not translate into better-supported complexes.** The primary bridge is null, with an interval wide enough to allow a modest advantage either way, and protein co-abundance favoured GLS. The secondary bridge is negative: zavg-map discoveries confirmed less often than GLS-map discoveries. Applied to both maps, the power aid accounts for roughly a quarter to two fifths of that gap. On present evidence the zavg map's value lies in its larger gene coverage. Why a better pair-level score yields no better map is untested; one possibility is that any reasonable clustering captures the gain on pair-rich complexes, while the extra coverage adds weaker modules.

**Complex-level context associations are matched by other coherent modules, not by any gene set.** Gene sets drawn from other co-dependency modules associated with lineages and drivers as often as complexes, and coherent modules of non-member genes did so on the units where they could be matched. Sets matched only for essentiality did so less often, though still well above 5%. The defensible reading is narrower than our first version's: a complex is not more context-associated than a coherent, essentiality-matched co-dependency module. Conditional on discovery, flagged complexes beat their own matched sets more often than selection alone predicts (46.4% against 29.1–36.4% across ten random atlases), but their effects are not larger. We have not compared our references with the nulls of earlier module-level atlases [Wainberg 2021; Chen 2023] and make no claim about how much of their signal is complex-specific; we suggest that such analyses report a matched-module reference.

**Replication is at the rate power predicts.** About half the lineage and 40–49% of the driver associations were confirmed in 338 held-out lines, close to what the post hoc power aid predicts. This is no evidence that discoveries fail to replicate beyond selection. It is also no evidence that they reflect biology rather than reproducible artefacts of one release. The complex-versus-gene benchmark is inconclusive, with a point estimate that favours the complex where many members are associated.

**Within complexes, 11 of 24 losses leave the complex-wide dependency within ±0.1 Chronos units, and none within ±0.05.** "Neutral" here means no change larger than the registered margin. The non-neutral calls are a registered control (ARID1A → ARID1B), a published partner dependency that passed only the rare-context route in the lockbox (EP300 → CREBBP [Ogiwara 2016]) and the PELO–HBS1L dependency after FOCAD loss [Prindle 2025]. Where something changes, it is a single paralogue or partner, in line with paralog buffering within complexes [De Kegel & Ryan 2019].

**What survives, and how far it goes.** The 254 strongest findings pass every registered check, with the leave-one-out check fully applicable to most of them. The examples we name are post hoc selections for presentation. For about two thirds of them a scale artefact cannot be excluded by the trend rule, and for a minority DEMETER2 does not agree. They are replicated, robustness-checked associations at the level of a module, candidates for testing outside DepMap, not demonstrations that the complex is the operative unit.

**Amendments and post hoc decisions.** Table 3 lists every change after the design freeze with its timing relative to outcome access (full record: Supplementary Note 14). The calibrations that changed our reading most were implemented after the discovery runs; the driver defect was found after the read; the robustness checks, both reporting labels and the revision analyses were run after the read, on A only.

| Defect handling: failed fits not evaluable; read as executed is primary; defect-sensitive calls carry no claim | After the lockbox read |
| Reporting rules: three H3b rates, BRIDGE-L headline, BENCH disclosure, power-aid reading | After the lockbox read; reporting only |
| Registered per-hit robustness computed; strongest findings fixed; descriptive annotations and labels | After the lockbox read; on A only |
| Revision analyses: matched-set references, spline trend with one artefact rule, DEMETER2 check, BRIDGE-L power | After peer review; post hoc, A only (L sample sizes and calls only) |
| Round-2 counts: H1 at a halved margin, alternative registry readings, atlas range | After the second review; post hoc, committed tables only |

**Limitations.** All dependencies come from one CRISPR release, and the lockbox shares its processing and Part 1's development data, so confirmation is not independent of release-level artefacts. The lockbox is small, so non-confirmation of small effects is often uninformative. "Complex" means a supported co-dependency module, not a curated complex. The non-member matched sets cover a quarter of the units, the scale rule cannot flag effects beyond the trend in its direction, and we did not rescale statistics by the never-expressed inflation. Analyses, reviews and rulings were carried out by AI agents under a human principal investigator; independent agent reviews reproduced every confirmatory number, but reviewers and producers share a model family (Supplementary Note 15).

**Conclusion.** A score selected on training complexes outperformed standard baselines on held-out pairs, but its map was not better supported by physical evidence, and its context associations replicated less often than a GLS map's. Complex-level context associations in DepMap are, in large part, shared by coherent co-dependency modules. Complex-specificity should be tested against matched modules and conditioned on discovery, not assumed.

### The locked method and the test protocol

zavg_BEF is described in the Part 1 repository, locked at commit c7f95ee. It is the equal-weight mean of three component z-scores computed on gene-standardised CRISPRGeneEffect profiles with the top 20 principal components removed and ridge whitening: first-order correlation, second-order neighbourhood cosine and a Laplacian-eigenmap cosine. It has no parameters fitted to labels; its configuration was selected on hu.MAP training pairs by complex-grouped cross-validation. Baselines are Part 1's signed Pearson and Spearman correlations and signed GLS t-statistics [Wainberg 2021]. A pre-declared secondary candidate and a post hoc PC-removed Pearson reference are in Supplementary Note 10.

The protocol (docs/TEST_PROTOCOL.md v1.2) was revised through three design-review rounds and frozen before the look. Pairs were canonicalised as unordered UniProt pairs; unmapped pairs (33,931 of 600,738) were kept at a floor score as one tied bottom block (ITT). Strata C1–C3 contain pairs with both, one or neither protein among training-positive proteins. Only ID files were scored. The primary metric is non-interpolated AP at natural prevalence [Saito & Rehmsmeier 2015; Davis & Goadrich 2006; Boyd 2013]; secondary metrics, three pre-specified operating rules and isotonic calibrators fitted on training pairs [Zadrozny & Elkan 2002; Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana 2005; Van Calster 2019] are listed in Supplementary Note 10. Test clusters are connected components of the test-positive graph [Blondel 2008].

The primary interval is Part 1's paired cluster bootstrap [Field & Welsh 2007; Owen 2007] (B = 2,000, seed 0; percentile intervals; two-sided p = 2·min(P(Δ ≤ 0), P(Δ ≥ 0))). With no replicate at or below 0, p is reported as < 0.001 and its Holm-adjusted value [Holm 1979, M3] as < 0.003 (Table 1). zavg_BEF "clearly beats" the baselines if, against each, the relative AP gain is at least 10% and macro per-cluster AP and AP without the largest component are at least 0.95× the baseline's, on S1. The freeze marker hashed the protocol, rules, code, package list and test files, and the look refused to run if any earlier look existed. These measures are procedural. One verifier re-implemented the protocol without importing the evaluation package.

### Firewalls, lockbox and contexts

Part 2 code may not read the hu.MAP files, test results or training labels; tests enforce this. BioPlex, CCLE proteomics, DEMETER2 and the lockbox are validation inputs only; the context builder read proteomics for a registered loss-of-function check before the map was committed. The universe U is the 1,345 lines that are not engineered or drug-adapted. The lockbox L (338 lines) was drawn by iterative stratification [Sechidis 2011, M9] on PatientID blocks using model labels only; no patient straddles A and L. L outcomes are read only through a ledgered function, once per registry entry. Contexts (MSI-H, loss of function with a functional check, hotspots, copy-number segments, fusions, sex, loss of Y, WGD, TP53, NRF2, lineage) were derived from DepMap Omics and Model.csv. Contexts with at least 12 A lines are confirmatory.

### Complex construction

zavg_BEF was recomputed on A with the locked configuration; on all U lines it reproduces Part 1 scores to ≤ 10⁻¹². S contains genes with effect < −0.5 in at least 1% of A lines. An edge joins genes with score ≥ z0 that are mutual top-k neighbours within S. Co-clustering frequencies over 20 PatientID-blocked 80% subsamples × 5 Leiden seeds were clustered with Leiden-CPM over a resolution grid [Traag 2019; Traag 2011; Fortunato & Barthélemy 2007; Monti 2003; Lancichinetti & Fortunato 2012; Jeub 2018; Zheng 2021 (HiDeF)]; persistent clusters were placed most-persistent-first, with nested clusters kept as sub-modules. Level-1 complexes have 3–60 members. (z0, k) was chosen from {4, 5, 6} × {10, 20} by split-half recovery over four PatientID-blocked splits. The GLS graph uses the same S, k and edge count, with weights quantile-matched. T1 requires within-cluster BioPlex edges above a degree- and essentiality-matched null (BH q < 0.05); T2 requires protein co-abundance above a size-matched null. The bridge pools within-cluster BioPlex precision within size bins {3–4, 5–8, 9–16, 17–60}, averages bins with pooled weights after coverage matching, and uses a protein-node bootstrap (B = 2,000, seed 5000). Rules were fixed on a toy simulation before any map was built (Supplementary Note 2).

### Association statistics

*Model.* The complex score is the mean clipped CRISPRGeneEffect of a complex's members, after removing the context gene(s), their in-complex paralogues and members within 10 Mb of a copy-number context gene. It is regressed on the context plus covariates by OLS: library, screen type and model type always; lineage for non-lineage contexts; mutation burden and MSIScore for mutation contexts; ploidy, WGD and TP53 status for copy-number contexts. Indicator levels with fewer than two lines are merged into the reference. The context coefficient is tested with HC3 errors [MacKinnon & White 1985, M4; Long & Ervin 2000, M5] and Satterthwaite df [Satterthwaite 1946, M6; Bell & McCaffrey 2002, M7]; a test needs at least 12 context and 12 reference A lines.

*Permutation and error control.* Tests with Benjamini–Bogomolov-adjusted q < 0.1 are re-tested by PatientID-blocked Freedman–Lane permutation [Winkler 2014] (10,000 permutations; a generalised Pareto tail [Knijnenburg 2009, M8] only below 10⁻⁴), and the permutation p re-enters the procedure. H3a and H3b use Benjamini–Bogomolov selective FDR [Benjamini & Bogomolov 2014, M1]: Simes family p-values [Simes 1986, M2], BH selection of families [Benjamini & Hochberg 1995] and BH at q·R/M within each of R selected families. The guarantee assumes independence across families and PRDS within them; contexts here share reference lines and overlap. H1 uses BH across events; the denominator was the evaluable m (24) rather than the registered 28, which moves PTEN's q from 0.044 to 0.051. H2 tests sub-modules stage-wise [Van den Berge 2017].

*H1.* For each genetic-loss event, β_P is the loss coefficient of the in-complex paralogue(s), and κ_N that of the mean of the other members, each aligned to its baseline direction. Classes need the effect outside a matched null of random member sets. Neutral means a 90% CI inside ±0.1 Chronos units [Schuirmann 1987]. Amendment A1 moved the 318 expression-silencing events to an exploratory family (H1S); amendment A2 then moved one of them, SMARCB1, into H1 as a genetic loss, leaving 317 H1S events with 321 tests. Member-resolved profiles were exported from A after the H1 lockbox read (Supplementary Note 8).

*Calibrations.* One hundred pseudo-contexts per direction, matched in size and lineage composition, ran with the discovery. The other registered calibrations were implemented and run once each on A after the discovery runs and before any lockbox read: matched gene sets (10 per complex, drawn from members of one other complex, essentiality within ±0.1, coherence within ±1.0 z); 100 dispersed never-expressed sets; a copy-number segment null; and 20 random splits per H2 sub-module.

### Controls

DESIGN numbers 22 controls (PC1–PC22); PC19 was registered as two entries (PC19a, PTEN loss with PIK3CB; PC19b, PIK3CA hotspot with PIK3CA), giving 23. Each ran once on A after the map and context freezes (Supplementary Table S1).

### Lockbox confirmation, BENCH and BRIDGE-L

A discovery is confirmed if its L effect has the same sign and one-sided p < 0.05 (same score and model; exact PatientID-blocked permutation when L has fewer than 20 context lines), for contexts with at least 5 context and 5 reference L lines. Rarer contexts take the rare-context route (leave-one-lineage-out, DEMETER2 on A lines and leave-one-line-out must all agree in sign). Each read was persisted with its hash committed at once. The single read used `scripts/51_lockbox_confirm.py`; its run log was not committed and is lost, so the loader call count is inferred from ledger invariants. BENCH compares t_L of the complex score with t_L of the member with the largest A |t| (g*) over discovery pairs; the registered criterion is the high-breadth tercile CI from a PatientID-blocked bootstrap over L lines (B = 2,000, seed 5100). BRIDGE-L compares size-standardised confirmation rates of GLS-map discoveries, made on A with the same machinery before the read, and zavg-map discoveries (bootstrap over hits, B = 2,000, seed 5200). The driver defect, its fix and the corrected relook are in Supplementary Note 3.

### Post hoc analyses

*Power aid.* For each hit on the lockbox route, power of the one-sided L test was computed from a noncentral t, taking the effect as the empirical-Bayes posterior of the A estimate (Tweedie's formula [Efron 2011]) and the SE from A scaled by line counts or from L (Supplementary Note 4). For BRIDGE-L the same function was applied per map using only committed L sample sizes and calls.

*Revision analyses (R-1 to R-4).* After peer review, four descriptive analyses were run on A with an intent committed first: matched sets drawn from genes outside every complex (coherent neighbourhoods, or essentiality only) and ten random atlases passed through the full discovery procedure; a natural-spline (4 df) trend of β on unit essentiality per lineage with one artefact rule; DEMETER2 refits; and BRIDGE-L power. They change no registered result. An independent agent checked them; it had read the results summary first, so the check was not blind. It reran scripts 59–61 and recomputed the numbers from the committed tables with its own code, but did not rerun the random atlases or the non-member test table (script 58) and reused, rather than re-implemented, the empirical-Bayes step of the power aid (Supplementary Note 17). For the second review round, three further counts were taken from committed tables only, with an intent committed first (R-5).

*Strongest findings.* The registered rule and its post hoc annotations are described in Supplementary Note 16.

### Registry and reproducibility

Every analysis is a committed, machine-readable registry entry; the engine refuses unregistered or modified entries and appends every run to a ledger. Amendments, errata and rulings are recorded with reasons in docs/registry/AMENDMENTS.md. Seeds are fixed throughout (Supplementary Note 9). Every headline number was re-derived by an agent that did not produce it. Figures and tables are generated from committed result files only (`scripts/fig_*.py`, `scripts/tab_*.py`), and `scripts/check_manuscript_numbers.py` checks every main-text number, and those of Supplementary Notes 16–17, against its cited source; the other Supplementary Notes are not yet fully machine-checked. On a laptop, one score run on A took 159 s and the lockbox read 2.6 h.
